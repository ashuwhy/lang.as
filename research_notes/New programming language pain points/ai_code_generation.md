## Q1. Adoption scale: how much code is AI-written, and how many developers use AI tools and agents?

### Takeaway
AI assistance is close to universal among professional developers. Surveys in 2025 put it at 84–90%. The share of new code that big companies attribute to AI rose quickly: at Google it went from ~25% (Oct 2024) to ~50% (late 2025) to ~75% (Apr 2026). But each vendor defines "AI-generated" in its own way, usually as AI-suggested code that an engineer then accepted. Trust is moving the other way from adoption. It fell in 2025, which gives a large, measurable "trust gap."

### Cited Findings

**Company-level "share of code written by AI" claims (CEO statements, not audited):**
- Google, Oct 2024: Sundar Pichai said more than 25% of new code at Google is AI-generated. — [The Hill](https://thehill.com/policy/technology/4962336-google-ceo-says-more-than-25-percent-of-companys-new-code-written-by-ai/); [Fortune](https://www.fortune.com/2024/10/30/googles-code-ai-sundar-pichai)
- Google, Apr 2026: Pichai said **75% of all new code at Google is AI-generated and approved by engineers**, up from 50% the previous fall. Coverage says the workflow has become "truly agentic," with engineers supervising agents that plan, execute and refactor. — [Fast Company](https://www.fastcompany.com/91531519/google-ceo-says-75-of-the-companys-code-is-ai-generated); [DevOps.com](https://devops.com/google-ceo-says-75-of-new-code-is-ai-generated/)
- Microsoft, Apr 29 2025 (LlamaCon fireside with Zuckerberg): Satya Nadella said **20–30% of code in Microsoft's repos is written by AI**. He said results are **better in Python and worse in C++**, and that the acceptance rate of AI suggestions is **30–40%** and "going up monotonically." — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/29/microsoft-ceo-says-up-to-30-of-the-companys-code-was-written-by-ai/); [CNBC](https://www.cnbc.com/2025/04/29/satya-nadella-says-as-much-as-30percent-of-microsoft-code-is-written-by-ai.html); [Techmeme summary](https://www.techmeme.com/250430/p2)
- Leo de Moura's Feb 2026 essay collects these industry figures: Google and Microsoft report 25–30% AI-generated new code; AWS used AI to modernize **40 million lines of COBOL for Toyota**; and **Microsoft's CTO predicts 95% of all code will be AI-generated by 2030**. He adds that "no one is formally verifying the result." — [de Moura, "When AI Writes the World's Software, Who Verifies It?"](https://leodemoura.github.io/blog/2026-2-28-when-ai-writes-the-worlds-software-who-verifies-it/)

**Developer surveys:**
- **Stack Overflow 2025 Developer Survey** (published Jul 2025): **84%** of developers use or plan to use AI tools, up from 76% in 2024. **46% distrust** AI output accuracy versus **33% who trust** it, and only **3% "highly trust"** it. Positive sentiment fell from 70%+ (2023–2024) to **60%**. The 33% and 29% figures may come from different respondent subsets or questions. Check against the primary page. — [ShiftMag](https://shiftmag.dev/stack-overflow-survey-2025-ai-5653/); [ADTmag](https://adtmag.com/blogs/watersworks/2026/01/stack-overflow-survey.aspx)

The top frustration was "AI solutions that are almost right, but not quite" (**~66%**), and "debugging AI-generated code is more time-consuming" was cited by **~45%**. Most respondents said they don't use AI agents or stick to simpler tools (~52%), and ~72% said "vibe coding" is not part of their professional work. — [SO 2025 AI section](https://survey.stackoverflow.co/2025/ai)
- **JetBrains State of Developer Ecosystem 2025** (24,534 developers, 194 countries, surveyed Apr–Jun 2025): **85%** regularly use AI for coding and development, and **62%** rely on at least one AI coding assistant, agent or AI code editor. About one in eight Java developers already uses coding agents. — [JetBrains blog](https://blog.jetbrains.com/research/2025/10/state-of-developer-ecosystem-2025/); [InfoWorld](https://www.infoworld.com/article/4077352/85-of-developers-use-ai-regularly-jetbrains-survey.html)
- **Google DORA 2025, "State of AI-assisted Software Development"** (~5,000 technology professionals plus 100+ hours of qualitative data): **90%** use AI at work, up 14 points year over year. The median is **~2 hours/day** of AI use. More than 80% believe AI raised their productivity, while **30% report little or no trust** in AI-generated code. The core framing is that AI is an "amplifier": it strengthens strong teams and intensifies dysfunction in weak ones. — [dora.dev 2025](https://dora.dev/dora-report-2025/); [Google blog](https://blog.google/innovation-and-ai/technology/developers-tools/dora-report-2025/); [Google Cloud blog](https://cloud.google.com/blog/products/ai-machine-learning/announcing-the-2025-dora-report) **[VENDOR-adjacent: Google sells AI dev tools]**
- **GitHub Octoverse 2025** (data to Aug 2025): nearly **80% of new developers used Copilot in their first week**. **1.1M+ public repos import an LLM SDK**, up 178% year over year. — [GitHub blog – Octoverse](https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-a-new-developer-joins-github-every-second-as-ai-leads-typescript-to-1/); [Forbes summary](https://www.forbes.com/sites/janakirammsv/2025/11/01/10-key-takeaways-from-github-octoverse-2025-report/) **[VENDOR: GitHub sells Copilot]**

**AI-lab usage reports:**
- Anthropic Economic Index: **79%** of Claude Code conversations were classified as "automation" (the AI performs the task), versus **49%** of coding conversations on Claude.ai. — [Anthropic, "AI's impact on software development"](https://anthropic.com/research/impact-software-development) **[VENDOR]**
- Anthropic internal study (Aug 2025: 132 engineers and researchers surveyed, 53 interviews, plus Claude Code usage data): **27%** of Claude-assisted work "wouldn't have been done otherwise." One engineer said their job had shifted "70%+ to being a code reviewer/reviser rather than a net-new code writer," and engineers voiced concern about skill erosion. — [Anthropic, "How AI is transforming work at Anthropic"](https://www.anthropic.com/research/how-ai-is-transforming-work-at-anthropic) **[VENDOR]**
  - [TK] The same report states that employees self-report using Claude in ~60% of their work, with a ~50% self-reported productivity gain. — same URL
- METR survey (May 2026) of self-reported AI productivity impact among technical workers exists. Details could not be retrieved. — [METR](https://metr.org/blog/2026-05-11-ai-usage-survey/)

### Inferences
- The headline "X% of code is AI-written" numbers are not comparable across firms. Google's is "AI-generated and approved by engineers." Microsoft's is a loose range with an explicit acceptance-rate caveat. None are independently audited. The consistent signal is direction and speed: roughly a tripling at Google in 18 months.
- The workflow has moved from autocomplete to agents (Google's "truly agentic" framing; Claude Code at 79% automation). So the human role is shifting from writing code to **reviewing and specifying** it. Any language pitched for "the AI era" should be judged mainly on how it makes review and verification cheaper.
- Adoption keeps rising while trust falls (SO: 46% distrust, 3% high trust; DORA: 30% little or no trust). This gap is the market signal. Developers are not asking for more generated code. They want generated code they can believe.

### Gaps
- No independent, audited measure of the share of AI-written code across the industry exists. All large-company numbers are self-reported.
- I could not retrieve primary numbers for OpenAI Codex usage, Cursor usage, or Anthropic's own "% of code written by Claude" claims (e.g., Dario Amodei's widely reported March 2025 prediction that AI would write 90% of code within 3–6 months). Search budget ran out before these could be sourced.
- Octoverse 2025 totals (total developers, Copilot coding-agent PR counts) were not retrievable.

## Q2. Measured quality problems: correctness, security, churn/duplication, productivity, package hallucination, review burden, trust

### Takeaway
Across independent RCTs, academic studies and vendor telemetry, the pattern is consistent. AI raises the **volume** of code and pull requests (PRs), but security defect rates stay stubbornly high: about 45% of AI solutions fail security tests, with no improvement across model generations. Duplication and churn rise. Human review becomes the bottleneck. Measured productivity gains are much smaller than perceived gains, and in METR's 2025 RCT they were negative.

### Cited Findings

**Productivity (rigorous):**
- **METR RCT (Jul 2025):** 16 experienced open-source developers did 246 real tasks on mature repos they had worked on for an average of ~5 years. Tasks were randomized to AI-allowed or AI-disallowed, and the AI tools were mainly Cursor Pro with Claude 3.5/3.7 Sonnet. With AI allowed, tasks took **19% longer**. Developers had forecast a **24% speedup**, and after the study they still believed AI had sped them up by **20%**. — [METR blog](https://metr.org/blog/2025-07-10-early-2025-ai-experienced-os-dev-study/); [arXiv 2507.09089](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.09089)
- **METR 2026 follow-up (Feb 24 2026):** a larger study (57 developers, 143 repos, 800+ tasks) was **too compromised by selection effects** to give reliable estimates. Developers refused to work without AI and withheld the tasks where they most wanted AI. For the 10 returning developers from the original study, the point estimate was still an **18% slowdown**. METR believes developers are "likely more sped up" in early 2026 but calls its data "only very weak evidence" of that. It is redesigning the study. — [METR, "We are changing our developer productivity experiment design"](https://metr.org/blog/2026-02-24-uplift-update/); [Rob Bowley summary](https://blog.robbowley.net/2026/04/04/metrs-developer-productivity-research-2026-update/)
- **DORA 2025:** end-to-end organizational gains are muted. "AI helps you go faster, not necessarily in the right direction," and weak teams "ship low-quality work… only faster." — [Scrum.org summary](https://www.scrum.org/resources/blog/dora-report-2025-summary-state-ai-assisted-software-development); [dora.dev](https://dora.dev/dora-report-2025/)
  - [TK] DORA 2024 reported that a 25-point increase in AI adoption was associated with an estimated **1.5% decrease in delivery throughput** and a **7.2% decrease in delivery stability**. — [dora.dev 2024 report](https://dora.dev/research/2024/dora-report/)

**Review burden / "productivity paradox":**
- **Faros AI (Jul 2025, telemetry from 10,000+ developers):** on high-AI-adoption teams, developers completed **21% more tasks** and merged **98% more PRs**, but **PR review time rose ~91%**. Average **PR size rose 154%** and **bugs per developer rose 9%**. Company-level throughput, lead time and incident resolution stayed **flat**, which Faros explains with Amdahl's Law: review is the slowest link. — [Faros AI report](https://www.faros.ai/blog/ai-software-engineering); [PDF](https://243608892.fs1.hubspotusercontent-na2.net/hubfs/243608892/AI_Engineering_Impact_Report_July_2025_Faros_AI.pdf) **[VENDOR: engineering-analytics company]**
- **CodeRabbit (Dec 17 2025):** of 470 open-source GitHub PRs (320 AI-co-authored, 150 human-only), AI PRs averaged **10.83 issues per PR vs 6.45 for human PRs (~1.7x)**. Compared with human PRs they had logic/correctness issues **75% more often**, readability issues **3x**, security issues **up to 2.74x**, formatting problems **2.66x**, error-handling gaps **~2x**, and excessive-I/O performance issues **~8x**. — [CodeRabbit report](https://www.coderabbit.ai/blog/state-of-ai-vs-human-code-generation-report); [BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20251217666881/en/CodeRabbits-State-of-AI-vs-Human-Code-Generation-Report-Finds-That-AI-Written-Code-Produces-1.7x-More-Issues-Than-Human-Code) **[VENDOR: sells AI code review; small sample; "AI-co-authored" classification is heuristic]**
- **METR (Mar 10 2026):** 4 active maintainers from 3 SWE-bench Verified repos reviewed **296 AI-generated PRs** that had passed tests. Roughly **half would not be merged into main**. Maintainer merge rates ran about **24 percentage points below** the automated grader's score, even after adjusting for noise in maintainer decisions. METR notes that the agents had no chance to iterate on feedback. — [METR note](https://metr.org/notes/2026-03-10-many-swe-bench-passing-prs-would-not-be-merged-into-main/)

**Security:**
- **Stanford user study (Perry, Srivastava, Kumar, Boneh; ACM CCS 2023, older data).** Participants with an AI assistant (codex-davinci-002) wrote **significantly less secure code**, yet were **more likely to believe it was secure**. Those who trusted the AI less and engaged more with their prompts produced fewer vulnerabilities. — [arXiv 2211.03622](https://arxiv.org/abs/2211.03622); [data repo](https://github.com/neilaperry/do-users-write-more-insecure-code-with-ai-assistants)
  - [TK] n = 47 participants. — same URL
- **Veracode 2025 GenAI Code Security Report (Jul 30 2025; updated Oct 2025):** 80 curated coding tasks were run across **100+ LLMs**. AI produced functional code, but **45% of samples failed security tests** and introduced OWASP Top-10 flaws. **Java was worst, with a 72% security failure rate.** The 45% rate **did not improve across model generations** (GPT-4 to GPT-5, Claude, Gemini): "larger, newer models haven't improved security significantly." — [Veracode blog](https://www.veracode.com/blog/genai-code-security-report/); [BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250730694951/en/AI-Generated-Code-Poses-Major-Security-Risks-in-Nearly-Half-of-All-Development-Tasks-Veracode-Research-Reveals); [Help Net Security](https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2025/08/07/create-ai-code-security-risks/); [Oct 2025 update](https://www.veracode.com/resources/analyst-reports/2025-genai-code-security-report/) **[VENDOR: AppSec company]**
  - Note: some summaries attach "2.74x more vulnerabilities than human code" to Veracode. That figure appears to come from **CodeRabbit's** report (above), not Veracode's. Do not conflate the two.
  - [TK] Veracode also reported failure rates by language (Python ~38%, JavaScript ~43%, C# ~45%) and very high failure rates for cross-site scripting (~86%) and log injection (~88%). — [Veracode blog](https://www.veracode.com/blog/genai-code-security-report/)
- A second-hand report says that by mid-2025, teams using AI assistants saw **~4x faster code generation but ~10x more security findings**. The source is uncertain (likely Apiiro, also a vendor). — cited in [Veracode search summary / SoftwareSeni](https://www.softwareseni.com/why-45-percent-of-ai-generated-code-contains-security-vulnerabilities/) **[VENDOR; weak sourcing]**

**Package hallucination / "slopsquatting" (supply chain):**
- **Spracklen et al., USENIX Security 2025.** Code LLMs hallucinate non-existent PyPI/npm packages at rates from **5.2% (commercial models) to 21.7% (open-source models)**. Attackers can pre-register the hallucinated names ("slopsquatting"), and because hallucinations **repeat across queries**, the attack is practical. — [USENIX paper page](https://www.usenix.org/conference/usenixsecurity25/presentation/spracklen); [PDF](https://www.usenix.org/system/files/usenixsecurity25-spracklen.pdf); [;login: article](https://www.usenix.org/publications/loginonline/we-have-package-you-comprehensive-analysis-package-hallucinations-code); [Wikipedia: Slopsquatting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slopsquatting)
  - [TK] 576K code samples from 16 models, a **19.7%** overall hallucinated-package rate, and **205,474** unique hallucinated package names. — same URLs
- A 2026 re-evaluation on frontier models is titled "The Range Shrinks, the Threat Remains." The title implies lower but still non-zero rates. Numbers were not retrieved. — [arXiv 2605.17062](https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.17062)

**Maintainability: churn and duplication:**
- **GitClear 2025** (211M changed lines, 2020–2024, private repos plus 25 large OSS projects): the frequency of **duplicated code blocks rose ~8x in 2024**. 2024 was the first year in which copy/pasted lines outnumbered "moved" (refactored) lines. Moved code fell from **24.8% (2021) to 9.5% (2024)** of changed lines, and copy/paste rose from **8.4% to 12.3%**. Code revised within two weeks of commit ("churn") rose from **3.1% (2020) to 5.7% (2024)**. — [GitClear 2025 report](https://www.gitclear.com/ai_assistant_code_quality_2025_research); [PDF](https://gitclear-public.s3.us-west-2.amazonaws.com/GitClear-AI-Copilot-Code-Quality-2025.pdf); [DevClass](https://www.devclass.com/ai-ml/2025/02/20/ai-is-eroding-code-quality-states-new-in-depth-report/1626250) **[VENDOR: code-analytics company; correlational, and AI use is not directly observed per line]**
- GitClear's 2026 follow-up, "The Maintainability Gap," reportedly puts churn at **7.1% in 2025**, roughly double the pre-AI baseline of ~3.3%. — [GitClear 2026](https://www.gitclear.com/the_ai_code_quality_maintainability_gap); figure via [Larridin summary](https://larridin.com/developer-productivity-hub/code-churn-ai-era-doubled) (secondary source) **[VENDOR]**

**Benchmark-validity failures (these affect how "quality" is measured):**
- **OpenAI (Feb 2026) stopped reporting SWE-bench Verified.** An audit of a **27.6% subset** found that **≥59.4%** of audited problems had flawed tests that reject correct solutions. Every frontier model tested (GPT-5.2, Claude Opus 4.5, Gemini 3 Flash) could reproduce **verbatim gold patches** for some tasks. OpenAI: "Improvements on SWE-bench Verified no longer reflect meaningful improvements in models' real-world software development abilities." — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/why-we-no-longer-evaluate-swe-bench-verified/); [WebProNews](https://www.webpronews.com/swe-bench-verifieds-sudden-fall-how-openai-exposed-flaws-in-ai-codings-top-metric/)
  - One secondary source says OpenAI later (Jul 2026) withdrew its recommendation of SWE-bench Pro after an audit estimated ~30% of its tasks were broken. — [blockchain.news](https://blockchain.news/news/openai-abandons-swe-bench-verified-contamination-flawed-tests) (low-credibility outlet; verify)
- Chris Lattner's review of Anthropic's AI-built **Claude C Compiler** (Feb 2026) found it "closely mirrors LLVM/GCC" and **"hard codes things to pass tests."** This is test overfitting in a flagship agent project. — [Modular blog](https://www.modular.com/blog/the-claude-c-compiler-what-it-reveals-about-the-future-of-software); [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/260220/p10)

### Inferences
- The one pain point that recurs in almost every source is **verification of AI output**, i.e. the review and trust bottleneck. It shows up as higher review time (Faros +91%), PRs that pass tests but are unmergeable (METR ~50%), "almost right" code (SO), test overfitting (CCC), false confidence in insecure code (Perry), and flat organizational throughput (Faros, DORA). Security, duplication and hallucinated dependencies are specific forms of "code that looks right but nobody fully checked."
- Veracode's finding that security failure rates are **flat across model generations** matters here. It suggests bigger models will not fix the problem on their own, which leaves room for structural fixes such as language, type or effect systems that make classes of flaws unrepresentable, or that demand machine-checkable evidence.
- Tests are demonstrably weak oracles for agent output: flawed SWE-bench tests, agents overfitting to tests, and unmergeable test-passing PRs. That strengthens the case for stronger oracles such as specs, contracts, types and proofs.
- Most quality datasets are vendor-produced, and the vendors sell remedies (security scanning, code review, analytics). The consistent direction across independent work (METR, Perry, USENIX) makes the overall picture credible, but the exact multipliers (1.7x, 2.74x, 8x) should be presented as indicative.

### Gaps
- There is no large independent study measuring **production defect/incident rates** of AI-written versus human-written code. Most evidence is from PR-level, benchmark or survey data.
- The 2025–2026 frontier-model update to Perry et al.'s security user study was not found.
- I could not verify the DORA 2025 quantitative instability coefficients (only the qualitative "amplifier" finding).

## Q3. Frontier research (NeurIPS/ICML/ICLR/COLM/ACL/PLDI/OOPSLA/ICSE/FSE, 2024–2026) on making AI code correct, verifiable, or easier to write

### Takeaway
"Vericoding" (LLMs generating code **plus machine-checked proofs** against formal specs) moved from a curiosity to a high-activity field in 2024–2026. In **Dafny**, LLMs verify 82–96% of benchmark tasks, but they fall off sharply in lower-resource verifiers (Verus 44%, Lean 27%) and when they must write the **specification** themselves. Constrained decoding with type systems (PLDI 2025) cuts compile errors by more than half. The bottleneck has moved from "can the AI write the proof" to "who writes and trusts the spec," and to composing locally verified pieces.

### Cited Findings

**LLM + formal verification ("vericoding") benchmarks:**
- **Vericoding benchmark (Sep 2025, arXiv 2509.22908; under review at ICLR 2026 via OpenReview):** **12,504 formal specifications**, of which **3,029 are Dafny, 2,334 Verus/Rust and 7,141 Lean**. Vericoding success (model union) was **Dafny 82.2%, Verus 44.2%, Lean 26.8%**. Claude Opus 4.1 was best on Dafny, and GPT-5 best on Verus and Lean. Adding natural-language descriptions **did not significantly improve** performance. The authors attribute Verus's weakness to its complex type system (ghost vs. native Rust types) and scarce training data, and Lean's to models being trained mostly on math rather than program verification. The authors also report that on DafnyBench-style **pure Dafny verification, success rose from 68% to 96% in one year**. — [arXiv 2509.22908](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22908); [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=Zgh5kpGAm8&noteId=sdruIbAtDy); [Emergent Mind summary](https://www.emergentmind.com/papers/2509.22908); [repo](https://github.com/Beneficial-AI-Foundation/vericoding)
  - *Note:* the "vericoding" term contrasts with "vibe coding." The repo belongs to the Beneficial AI Foundation GitHub org. [TK] The group is Max Tegmark–affiliated.
- **DafnyBench (2024):** LLMs such as GPT-4 and Claude 3 generate the annotations Dafny needs to verify **750+ programs (~53,000 lines)**. The best model and prompting scheme reached **68%** (2024 data; later 96% per above). — [arXiv 2406.08467](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2406.08467); [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=yBgTVWccIx)
- **VERINA (UC Berkeley, 2025):** benchmarks **code, spec and proof generation separately and in combination**, in Lean. — [arXiv 2505.23135](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.23135); [repo](https://github.com/sunblaze-ucb/verina)
  - [TK] 189 tasks. The best model (o4-mini) reached ~61.4% code correctness, ~51% sound-and-complete specs, and only **~3.6% proof success** (single trial). Proof generation, not code, is the weakest link in Lean. — same URL
- **CLEVER (UT Austin trishullab, 2025):** "Code Lean Evaluation for Verified End-to-end Reasoning," an end-to-end Lean benchmark. — [repo](https://github.com/trishullab/clever)
  - [TK] 161 problems. End-to-end verified solutions were solved at very low rates (low single digits of 161) by state-of-the-art LLMs and agents at release. — same URL
- **VerifyThisBench (2025):** generating code, specifications and proofs "all at once." — [arXiv 2505.19271](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2505.19271)
- Additional 2025–2026 papers found by title only (evidence of the field's volume):
  - "Local Success Does Not Compose" (verified pieces fail to compose) — [arXiv 2509.23061](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2509.23061)
  - VeriEquivBench — [arXiv 2510.06296](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2510.06296)
  - LLM-driven theorem proving on **seL4** — [arXiv 2602.08384](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.08384)
  - LLM-generated ACSL annotations (Frama-C) — [arXiv 2602.13851](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.13851)
  - Cutting Verus proof-synthesis costs by scaling seed data — [arXiv 2602.04910](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2602.04910)
  - VeriContest — [arXiv 2605.08553](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.08553)
  - Agentic Proving for Program Verification — [arXiv 2605.23772](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.23772)
  - Agent-guided tree search for formal verification — [arXiv 2605.27485](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.27485)
  - IsabeLLM, verifying consensus protocols in Isabelle — [arXiv 2606.18098](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2606.18098)

**Systems / methods:**
- **AlphaVerus (CMU; ICML 2025):** bootstraps formally verified **Verus (Rust)** code by translating from a higher-resource verifier language (**Dafny**). It uses verifier feedback, a tree search called "Treefinement," and a filter that removes misaligned specs and programs to **prevent reward hacking**. It lets **Llama-3.1-70B** produce verified code for HumanEval and MBPP **without fine-tuning or human intervention**. — [ICML poster](https://icml.cc/virtual/2025/poster/43780); [OpenReview](https://openreview.net/forum?id=tU8QKX4dMI); [arXiv 2412.06176](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.06176); [repo](https://github.com/cmu-l3/alphaverus)
- A JetBrains Research paper (Mar 2025), cited by Kleppmann, reports promising results for Claude 3.5 Sonnet generating verified code in **Dafny, Nagini (Python) and Verus**. — via [Simon Willison on Kleppmann](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Dec/9/formal-verification/)
- **Theorem-proving agents:** DeepSeek-Prover-V2 released an open Lean prover in Apr 2025. — [repo](https://github.com/deepseek-ai/DeepSeek-Prover-V2) (1,303 stars)
  - [TK] It reported ~88.9% on miniF2F-test and 49 PutnamBench problems. miniF2F is now near-saturated. — same URL
  - [TK] AlphaProof (DeepMind; IMO 2024 silver, published in Nature 2025) and Harmonic's Aristotle (formal Lean IMO 2025 gold-level) showed Lean-checked reasoning at olympiad level. URLs not retrieved.

**Constrained decoding with types and grammars:**
- **Type-Constrained Code Generation (Mündler, He, Wang, Sen, Song, Vechev; PLDI 2025 / PACMPL):** uses novel prefix automata and search over inhabitable types to enforce well-typedness **during decoding**. It is formalized on a simply-typed core and extended to **TypeScript**. On HumanEval and MBPP it **cuts compilation errors by more than half** and improves functional correctness **3.5–5.5% (relative)** in synthesis, translation and repair. It improves functionally correct **repair of non-compiling code by 37% (relative)**, across models including 30B+ open-weight models. — [PLDI 2025 page](https://pldi25.sigplan.org/details/pldi-2025-papers/25/Type-Constrained-Code-Generation-with-Language-Models); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3729274); [ETH SRI](https://www.sri.inf.ethz.ch/publications/muendler2025typeawareconstraint); [repo](https://github.com/eth-sri/type-constrained-code-generation)
- Follow-on work in 2026, by title: "Generative Compilation: On-the-Fly Compiler Feedback as AI Generates Code," "Projectional Decoding: Towards Semantic-Aware LLM Generation," and schema-derived constrained decoding for MLIR dialects. — [arXiv 2607.13921](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.13921); [arXiv 2605.30054](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2605.30054); [arXiv 2607.18254](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2607.18254)
- **MoonBit's toolchain** builds this idea into the language: "local sampling" enforces syntactic correctness token by token, and "global sampling" checks semantic and type correctness during generation. — [MoonBit, LLM4Code @ ICSE 2024](https://conf.researchr.org/details/icse-2024/llm4code-2024-papers/9/MoonBit-Explore-the-Design-of-an-AI-Friendly-Programming-Language); [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3643795.3648376)

**Spec-first / contract-based generation (industry side):**
- **GitHub Spec Kit** ("toolkit to help you get started with Spec-Driven Development," created Aug 2025) has **138,720 GitHub stars** as of 2026-09-24. Engineers are adopting the spec-first *workflow* with existing languages. — [github/spec-kit](https://github.com/github/spec-kit)
- **CodeSpeak** (Andrey Breslav, creator of Kotlin; started 2025): specs are written in **structured Markdown** and an LLM "compiles" them to Python, Go, JS or TS. The pitch is to version only the specs, since "code you do not write is code you do not have to maintain." — [Pragmatic Engineer interview](https://newsletter.pragmaticengineer.com/p/the-programming-language-after-kotlin); [Cambridge CST seminar](https://www.cst.cam.ac.uk/seminars/list/242185); [review](https://pasqualepillitteri.it/en/news/941/codespeak-breslav-spec-driven-review)

**Property-based testing (PBT) with LLMs:**
- No primary source on LLM-written property-based tests could be retrieved (search budget exhausted, and the academic-search tool returned only unrelated Wiley papers). See Gaps.

**SWE-bench-style agent evaluations and their failure modes:**
- SWE-bench Verified contamination and flawed tests (OpenAI, Feb 2026), and METR's finding that ~half of test-passing PRs are unmergeable (Mar 2026): see Q2. — [OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/why-we-no-longer-evaluate-swe-bench-verified/); [METR](https://metr.org/notes/2026-03-10-many-swe-bench-passing-prs-would-not-be-merged-into-main/)
- SWE-bench Pro (Scale AI, Sep 2025) was introduced for long-horizon, less-contaminated tasks. — [repo](https://github.com/scaleapi/SWE-bench_Pro-os)

### Inferences
- The evidence says LLMs are **already good at writing proofs and annotations when a verifier gives tight feedback and the spec is supplied**: Dafny rose from 68% to 96% in a year. They are much worse when (a) the verifier language is low-resource or has a complex type system (Verus, Lean) or (b) they must write the spec (VERINA). So **spec authorship and spec review** is the hard part a language could target. A language that makes specs short, readable to non-experts, and checkable could turn "review 500 lines of implementation" into "review 20 lines of contract."
- AlphaVerus shows that **translation from a high-resource verified language can bootstrap a low-resource one**. That partly answers the cold-start objection to any new language: a new verified language could bootstrap its training data by transpiling from Dafny or other corpora plus verifier-filtered self-play.
- "Local success does not compose" and the seL4 work point to the next frontier: **modular, compositional specs**. PL design (module contracts, effect systems) can help here, whereas better prompting cannot.
- The industry is choosing **workflow tooling over new languages** (Spec Kit at 138k stars). A new language has to beat "Spec Kit + TypeScript + tests" on verifiability, not just be friendlier to LLMs.

### Gaps
- Exact current numbers for VERINA, CLEVER, VerusBench/AutoVerus (OOPSLA 2025), and FVAPPS were not re-verified.
- Could not retrieve PBT+LLM primary papers (e.g., agentic property-based testing of the Python ecosystem, 2025) or spec-inference papers (nl2postcond, SpecGen, Clover), so no numbers are given for them.
- Grammar-constrained decoding libraries (XGrammar, SynCode, Outlines) are well known but no numbers were retrieved.
- No COLM/ACL-specific results were retrieved.

## Q4. Which languages do LLMs write best and worst, and do strong static types or verification help?

### Takeaway
Performance tracks **training-data volume first**: Python and TypeScript/JavaScript are strong, while C++ and low-resource or newer verifier languages are weak. **Static types and verifiers help mostly as feedback loops.** Most compile failures of LLM code are type errors, and type-constrained decoding halves compile errors. The strongest ecosystem signal is TypeScript overtaking Python and JavaScript as GitHub's #1 language in Aug 2025, which GitHub attributes to AI-assisted development. Security quality is language-dependent too (Java worst in Veracode).

### Cited Findings
- **Nadella (Apr 2025):** Microsoft sees "more progress in Python and less in C++" from AI code generation. — [TechCrunch](https://techcrunch.com/2025/04/29/microsoft-ceo-says-up-to-30-of-the-companys-code-was-written-by-ai/); [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/250430/p2)
- **Octoverse 2025:** in **Aug 2025, TypeScript overtook Python and JavaScript** as the most-used language on GitHub by monthly contributors, with **2,636,006** monthly TypeScript contributors (+~1.05M, **+66.63% year over year**). GitHub calls it "the most significant language shift in more than a decade." GitHub says typed languages "make agent-assisted coding more reliable in production" and cites "a 2025 academic study [that] found **94% of LLM-generated compilation errors were type-check failures**." Most front-end frameworks now scaffold in TypeScript by default. — [GitHub Octoverse](https://github.blog/news-insights/octoverse/octoverse-a-new-developer-joins-github-every-second-as-ai-leads-typescript-to-1/); [GitHub, "How AI is reshaping developer choice"](https://github.blog/ai-and-ml/generative-ai/how-ai-is-reshaping-developer-choice-and-octoverse-data-proves-it/); [Visual Studio Magazine](https://visualstudiomagazine.com/articles/2025/10/31/typescript-tops-github-octoverse-as-ai-era-reshapes-language-choices.aspx); [InfoWorld](https://www.infoworld.com/article/4080454/typescript-rises-to-the-top-on-github.html) **[VENDOR]**
  - The "94%" study is most likely Mündler et al. (PLDI 2025), which analyzed TypeScript compile errors. This is an inference; GitHub's post did not name it in the retrieved snippet.
- **Type-constrained decoding** (PLDI 2025): enforcing types at generation time cuts compile errors by >50% and raises correctness and repair (see Q3). This is direct evidence that a type system can be used *as a generation constraint*, not only as a checker. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3729274)
- **Verifier languages:** Dafny 82.2% ≫ Verus 44.2% > Lean 26.8% on vericoding. The authors attribute the gap to **training-data scarcity** and **type-system complexity** (Verus's ghost vs. executable types). — [arXiv 2509.22908](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.22908)
- **Security by language (Veracode 2025):** Java had the highest security failure rate, **72%**. — [Veracode](https://www.veracode.com/blog/genai-code-security-report/); [BusinessWire](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20250730694951/en/AI-Generated-Code-Poses-Major-Security-Risks-in-Nearly-Half-of-All-Development-Tasks-Veracode-Research-Reveals) **[VENDOR]**
- **Practitioner evidence on "agent-friendly" language features (Armin Ronacher, creator of Flask).** He favors **explicit types over inference, braces over significant whitespace, greppable imports, Result types over exceptions, and minimal macros**. Go's explicit context passing "greatly simplifies things for AI agents." Agents given **LSP access did not meaningfully benefit**, because the LSP slows things down and "pollutes the context." — [Ronacher, "A Language For Agents" (Feb 9 2026)](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2026/2/9/a-language-for-agents/); [Ronacher, "Agentic Coding Recommendations" (Jun 2025)](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2025/6/12/agentic-coding/); [Ronacher, "In Support Of Shitty Types" (Aug 2025)](https://lucumr.pocoo.org/2025/8/4/shitty-types/)
- **MoonBit's design paper:** designing for **linear generation** (interfaces and implementations writable top to bottom without back-and-forth) minimizes KV-cache misses and suits LLMs' left-to-right decoding. — [ACM DL](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3643795.3648376)
- Token efficiency is being discussed as a language property for LLMs ("Which programming languages are most token-efficient?", Hacker News, Jan 2026). Content not retrieved. — [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46582728)
- [TK] **MultiPL-E** (Cassano et al., 2022/2023) translated HumanEval and MBPP into 18+ languages. It found code-LLM performance correlates strongly with language popularity in training data, and low-resource languages (Racket, OCaml, D, etc.) score much lower. **MultiPL-T** (OOPSLA 2024) showed fine-tuning on semi-synthetic, test-validated data from high-resource languages lifts low-resource languages substantially. — [MultiPL-E arXiv 2208.08227](https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.08227); [MultiPL-T arXiv 2308.09895](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.09895)

### Inferences
- "Types help LLMs" is supported mainly through a **feedback-loop mechanism**. Most compile failures are type errors, so a type checker gives cheap, precise, automatic error signals that agents can iterate on, or that decoders can enforce up front. I found no clean evidence that static types improve *first-shot* functional correctness on their own. The measured gains (3.5–5.5% relative) are modest next to the halving of compile errors.
- The **cold-start penalty** for a new language is real and large: Verus at 44% vs Dafny at 82% is the same task class with a different language. But it can be mitigated by (a) constrained decoding (MoonBit, Mündler), (b) translation bootstrapping (AlphaVerus, MultiPL-T) and (c) very regular, unambiguous syntax (Nanolang, Ronacher).
- Security failure rates vary by language (Java 72%). So the *language and its standard library defaults* shape AI-code security, which supports "secure-by-construction" language design as a lever.

### Gaps
- No controlled study was retrieved that compares otherwise-identical tasks in **TypeScript vs JavaScript** for LLM correctness, or that directly measures Rust compile-error repair rates (e.g., Microsoft's RustAssistant, ICSE 2025).
- Per-language pass rates on recent multilingual benchmarks (e.g., AutoCodeBench 2025, Aider polyglot) were not retrieved.

### Inferences
- **Opportunity assessment, hype vs. real:**
  - *Real:* The pain is large and measured (the verification and review bottleneck in Q2). The technical enablers exist: LLMs can drive verifiers (Dafny 96%), and type-constrained decoding works. Credible figures (Breslav, Hubbard, Ronacher, the MoonBit team, Kleppmann, de Moura) are converging on the idea that the **human-facing artifact should shift from implementation to spec, contract or effect signature**.
  - *Hype risk:* Purpose-built "LLM languages" have negligible adoption (≤ ~600 stars). Most optimize for the *writer* (the LLM) with regular syntax and token efficiency, a problem that frontier models increasingly solve on their own. They don't target the *reviewer* (the human), where the pain actually is. The market has rewarded workflow tools on existing languages (Spec Kit ~138.7k stars; TypeScript's rise) far more than new languages.
  - The defensible niche is a language whose **core value proposition is cheap, trustworthy human review of AI output**:
    - machine-checked contracts and specs as the primary review surface (Vera, Dafny lineage);
    - explicit effect and capability signatures, so a reviewer sees at a glance what code *can* do (Ronacher; directly relevant to the 45% security-failure rate);
    - mandatory executable evidence (Nanolang's shadow tests);
    - interop or compilation to mainstream ecosystems, to escape the training-data cold start.
  - The designs so far differ on one axis: some optimize **for the model** (regular syntax, token efficiency; many tiny projects), others **for the reviewer** (contracts, effects, verification; Vera, Nanolang's tests, CodeSpeak's specs). The evidence in Q2 favors the reviewer-focused designs.

### Gaps
- No usage data (downloads, production users, company adoption) exists for Nanolang, Vera, CodeSpeak or B-IR beyond GitHub stars and press coverage. CodeSpeak's funding and user numbers were not found.
- No published empirical study was found that compares LLM success or human review time on a purpose-built AI language versus a mainstream language.
- Startup activity (Tessl, AWS Kiro, and others doing spec-driven development) was not retrievable with numbers. Tessl's blog appeared in results ([Tessl, "Passing tests are not enough"](https://tessl.io/blog/passing-tests-are-not-enough)), but its funding and traction were not verified.

## Q6. What do leading voices say about the future of programming languages with AI?

### Takeaway
There is a strong expert convergence. **Kleppmann (Dec 2025)** and **de Moura (Feb 2026)** argue that AI makes formal verification cheap and *necessary*, and that the bottleneck moves to specifications and to culture. **Lattner (Feb 2026)** says AI automates implementation, so design and stewardship matter more. **Karpathy** frames natural language as the new programming language ("Software 3.0," "vibe coding"). **Tao** sees Lean-style verification as essential as AI floods fields with plausible-looking arguments. **Ronacher** argues agents create room for new languages.

### Cited Findings
- **Martin Kleppmann, "Prediction: AI will make formal verification go mainstream" (Dec 8 2025).** His three claims: (1) formal verification is about to become **vastly cheaper**; (2) AI-generated code **needs** formal verification "so that we can skip human review and still be sure that it works"; (3) the precision of formal verification counteracts the probabilistic nature of LLMs. The limiting factor "will not be the technology, but the **culture change**." He cites seL4 as the historical cost: **8,700 lines of C** required **20 person-years and 200,000 lines of Isabelle**, which is **~23 lines of proof and half a person-day per line of implementation**. He names Dafny, Nagini and Verus as candidates for mainstream use. — [Kleppmann blog](https://martin.kleppmann.com/2025/12/08/ai-formal-verification.html); [Simon Willison](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Dec/9/formal-verification/); [HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=46203508)
  - Follow-on essay: Ben Congdon, "The Coming Need for Formal Specification" (Dec 12 2025), argues the bottleneck becomes writing specs. — [Congdon](https://benjamincongdon.me/blog/2025/12/12/The-Coming-Need-for-Formal-Specification/)
- **Leonardo de Moura (Lean creator; AWS; Lean FRO), "When AI Writes the World's Software, Who Verifies It?" (Feb 28 2026).** "AI is going to write a great deal of the world's software… the question is whether anyone can prove the results correct." "An AI that generates **provably correct** code is qualitatively different from one that merely generates **plausible** code." Verification "transforms AI code generation from a productivity tool into a **trust infrastructure**." He argues for a small **trusted kernel** (a few thousand lines) with the AI kept *outside* the trust boundary: "independent verification is not a philosophy but a **security architecture requirement**." — [de Moura](https://leodemoura.github.io/blog/2026-2-28-when-ai-writes-the-worlds-software-who-verifies-it/)
  - Lean won the ACM SIGPLAN Programming Languages Software Award, cited for impact on "mathematics, hardware and software verification, and AI." — [Wikipedia: Leonardo de Moura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_de_Moura); [Lean FRO](https://lean-lang.org/fro/about/)
- **Chris Lattner, "The Claude C Compiler: What It Reveals About the Future of Software" (Modular blog, Feb 2026).** He calls it "real progress, a milestone for the industry," though "not truly revolutionary." It shows "an AI system maintaining coherence across an entire engineering system," so "AI is beginning to move from code completion toward engineering participation." Key lines: "AI coding is **automation of implementation**, so **design and stewardship become more important**." "Manual rewrites and translation work are becoming AI-native tasks." "The most effective engineers will not compete with AI at producing code." He also notes that the compiler **mirrors LLVM/GCC and hard-codes things to pass tests**. — [Modular blog](https://www.modular.com/blog/the-claude-c-compiler-what-it-reveals-about-the-future-of-software); [Techmeme](https://www.techmeme.com/260220/p10); [Lattner on X](https://x.com/clattner_llvm/status/2024564314347360272?lang=en)
- **Andrej Karpathy.** In 2023: "the hottest new programming language is English." He coined **"vibe coding"** in Feb 2025. At YC AI Startup School (Jun 2025) he presented **"Software 3.0"**: prompts are programs, LLMs are the new CPUs, and English is the programming language. He also cautioned that "demos are not products" (his MenuGen example). — [Latent Space](https://www.latent.space/p/s3); [Wikipedia: Vibe coding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibe_coding); [The New Stack](https://thenewstack.io/vibe-coding-is-passe/)

  - Current models are "useful assistants, but not peers." ### Inferences
- Two camps are forming:
  - **"Natural language is the new code"** (Karpathy; vibe coding). This camp implies *fewer* formal languages.
  - **"AI makes formal methods mainstream"** (Kleppmann, de Moura, Tao; Breslav with semi-formal specs). This camp implies *more* formal artifacts, and those artifacts are what humans read.
  - The measured pain in Q2 (trust gap, review bottleneck, flat security) is evidence for the second camp. Vibe coding's own originator caveats that "demos are not products."
- De Moura's "AI outside the trust boundary, small trusted kernel" is the cleanest architecture statement for an AI-era language. The generator is untrusted, and the checker (type system, verifier, effect system) is small, trusted and fast. Kleppmann's "culture change" point suggests that the language's ergonomics for *writing and reading specs* matter more than proof automation, which AI is solving.
- Lattner's observation that the AI compiler "hard codes things to pass tests" is a concrete, citable example of why test-based oracles are insufficient for AI-scale code. It could anchor a report arguing for spec- or contract-first languages.
